# Privacy Budget Report

Generated by `budget.py` (Chapter 4). Pure Python, standard library only. All composition totals are computed from the formulas, not asserted.

## Scenario 1: basic composition, and the accountant refusing a query

- Total budget: epsilon 1.0
- Queries run: 10 counting queries at epsilon 0.1 each
- Basic composition total: 1.0
- Spent: 1.0; remaining: 0.0
- Eleventh query at epsilon 0.1 allowed? NO, refused

The refusal is the feature. Without an accountant, the eleventh query would silently push the total to 1.1 and the published guarantee would be wrong. Real deployments (the Census Bureau, Apple, Google) track the same ledger, just with more queries.

## Scenario 2: advanced composition, where it wins

- 100 queries at epsilon 0.01 each, delta' = 1e-05
- Basic composition total: 1.00
- Advanced composition total: 0.49 (with failure probability at most 1e-05)

With 100 small queries, advanced composition roughly halves the bill: 0.49 instead of 1.0. The total grows like the square root of the number of queries instead of linearly. The price is a tiny delta: with probability at most 1 in 100,000 the guarantee can fail, which is why deployments pick delta well below 1/n, where n is the number of people in the data.

## Scenario 3: advanced composition, where it loses

- 10 queries at epsilon 0.1 each, delta' = 1e-05
- Basic composition total: 1.00
- Advanced composition total: 1.62

Here advanced composition is worse: 1.62 instead of 1.0. The square-root term only pays off when each epsilon is small and there are many queries. Never apply a formula blindly; compute both and take the smaller total.

## Scenario 4: parallel composition on disjoint groups

- Three age bands: 18-29, 30-49, 50+
- One counting query per band, epsilon 0.5 each
- Naive sum: 1.5
- Parallel composition total: 0.5

Because no person appears in more than one band, the three queries together cost only 0.5, not 1.5. Splitting queries across disjoint groups is free privacy. It is also the reason the Census publishes per-geography counts: the geographies are disjoint, so the budget stretches further.

## Scenario 5: group privacy

A mechanism that is epsilon-DP for one person is k*epsilon-DP for a group of k people. At epsilon 1.0 per person:

| group size k | effective epsilon for the group |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0 |
| 2 | 2.0 |
| 5 | 5.0 |
| 10 | 10.0 |

A household of 5 gets epsilon 5.0 of protection from a mechanism that gives epsilon 1.0 to an individual. Differential privacy protects individuals; protecting groups costs proportionally more. If your threat model includes households or families, budget for it.

## The rule to take away

Every query spends budget. Basic composition adds the epsilons. Advanced composition can cut the bill when queries are many and small, at the price of a tiny delta. Parallel composition is free across disjoint groups. Keep a ledger, refuse queries that bust the budget, and publish the total epsilon alongside the results: a statistic without its epsilon is an un-auditable claim.
