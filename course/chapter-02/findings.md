## Exercise B: Purpose-limitation test

### Customer-service chatbot (support chat logs)

1. Original purpose (one sentence): The chat logs were collected to resolve that customer's support request.
2. Training purpose (one sentence): The model will learn from the logs to answer future customers' questions.
3. Article 6(4) factors:
   - Link between the purposes: Related but not the same. Both are customer service, but one helps this customer now and the other builds a product for all future customers.
   - Context of collection: Customers gave their details to get help, not to train a model, so they would not reasonably expect their chat to become training data.
   - Nature of the data: The logs include names, email addresses, order history, and home addresses, which are direct identifiers, and sometimes payment details.
   - Consequences for the person: If the model memorizes a log and repeats an address or order detail to another customer, that is a real leak, and the customer loses control of data they shared in confidence.
   - Safeguards you would apply: Pseudonymize the logs first, meaning replace names, emails, and addresses with tokens, minimize by dropping order numbers and payment details and keeping only the question and answer pattern, and delete the raw logs after training
4. Verdict (compatible / compatible with safeguards / not compatible): compatible with safeguards. Not a clean compatible, because the purposes differ and customers never expected training use. Not incompatible either, because the link is genuine and the safeguards close the gap. This middle verdict is the most common real answer in governance work.

## Exercise C: Article 10(2) mapping for TinyStories

One line of evidence, or one line admitting a gap, for each practice.

- (a) design choices:  The dataset was designed as synthetic short stories in a 3 to 4 year old's vocabulary, generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, so a small model can learn fluent English. Evidence: Eldan and Li 2023.
- (b) collection and origin: Nothing was collected from people. The stories were generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 from prompt templates and word lists, and the generation process is described in the paper. Our 50 row audit found no real personal data; the 9 name matches were all false positives.
- (c) preparation operations: The paper documents how stories were generated, but there is no separate published cleaning, annotation, or filtering log. Our 50 row sample found 0 duplicates, which supports a clean set but does not replace a provider log. Gap, partial.
- (d) assumptions: No stated assumptions. The paper implies that simple vocabulary stories are a good stand in for learning English, but it never states what the data is supposed to measure or represent. Gap.
- (e) availability and suitability: The dataset is public on Hugging Face with about 2.1 million stories, far more than Chapter 5 needs, and it was built for small models, so it suits the intended use. The license conflict limits suitability for commercial use.
- (f) bias examination: No bias examination is documented in the dataset card or the paper. Gap.
- (g) bias mitigation: No mitigation measures are documented, because no biases were examined in the first place. Gap.
- (h) gaps and shortcomings: Four gaps to close before shipping. State the representation assumptions, run a bias check on the generated stories, document any mitigations found, and resolve the license conflict by following the stricter non-commercial term. Also publish a cleaning log.

## What I would do differently next time
The takeaway to write under the list: the audit found three clean gaps, (d), (f), and (g), plus the license conflict and the missing cleaning log. A high-risk provider would have to close all of them before shipping. At toy scale, naming them in (h) with a fix for each is exactly the discipline Article 10(2) asks for.
