## 1. The one picture: every dataset has a chain of custody

A dataset is not a file. It is a chain:

```
SOURCE (who made it, from what)  ->  LICENSE (what you may do with it)
   ->  COLLECTION (how it was gathered)  ->  YOUR USE (what you will do with it)
```

If any link in that chain is missing, you have a governance gap, not a technical problem.
A model trained on data you had no right to use is a legal problem that no amount of
accuracy can fix. That is why data governance comes before training in this course, and
why the EU AI Act puts it in Article 10, the first substantive duty for high-risk systems.

Three questions organize the whole chapter:

1. **May I use it?** (license and lawful basis)
2. **Do I know what is in it?** (datasheet and content checks)
3. **Does my use match the purpose it was collected for?** (purpose limitation)

## 2. May I use it? Licenses are permissions, not labels

A license is a permission slip. It says who may use the data, for what, and under what
conditions. Common ones you will meet:

- **CC BY 4.0:** use it, including commercially, if you give credit. Simple and permissive.
- **CC BY-SA 4.0:** use it, including commercially, if you give credit AND share anything
  you build on it under the same license. The "share-alike" part is the catch: it can force
  your downstream work to be open too.
- **CC BY-NC 4.0:** use it for non-commercial purposes only. Training a model you later
  sell, or a model inside a paid product, is commercial use. Research in this course is
  non-commercial, so NC licenses are fine here, but they are not fine for a startup.
- **Apache 2.0 / MIT:** permissive software-style licenses. Fine for most uses, with
  attribution and a copy of the license.
- **CDLA Sharing 1.0:** a data-specific license that requires you to share results and
  data derived from it under the same terms.
- **No license stated:** this is the most common trap. No license does not mean "free to
  use." It means no permission has been granted. The default in copyright law is that you
  may not copy or use the work. Treat an unlicensed dataset as "research use only, and
  document the gap."

**The rule for this course:** if you cannot find a license, write "license: not declared"
in your audit, note what you did to look, and limit your use to non-commercial research.
Never write a license you did not verify.

### The TinyStories finding (verified November 15, 2026)

TinyStories (`roneneldan/TinyStories`) is the dataset you will train on in Chapter 5, so
audit it now. Here is what a check of its Hugging Face page and API on November 15, 2026
found:

- The dataset card describes synthetic short stories generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4,
  from the paper by Eldan and Li (arXiv:2305.07759).
- **The metadata declares CDLA Sharing 1.0 as the license**, but the card text itself
  never mentions licensing at all. The permission lives in a metadata field, not in
  any sentence a reader would see.
- A large independent audit of dataset licensing (Longpre et al., Nature Machine
  Intelligence, 2024) found a conflicting label for TinyStories: CC BY-NC 4.0. Two
  sources, two different answers for the same dataset.
- The stories are outputs of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, so the terms under which those models
  were used add a second layer of conditions on top of whatever the dataset claims.

That is not a reason to abandon the dataset. It is a reason to document the conflict,
follow the stricter of the two labels (CC BY-NC 4.0 means non-commercial use only),
and keep your use inside non-commercial research, which is what this course is. The
finding itself is the lesson: a dataset used by hundreds of published models has no
clean, single license statement. If you had skipped the audit, you would never have
known.

Contrast that with SQuAD (`rajpurkar/squad`): its dataset card states plainly,
"The dataset is distributed under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license." One line, verifiable, no
ambiguity. That is what good looks like.

## 3. Do I know what is in it? The datasheet

A **datasheet for datasets** is a standard document that answers, in one place: why the
dataset was created, what it contains, how it was collected, and what it should not be
used for. The format comes from Gebru et al., "Datasheets for Datasets" (2021). It is the
single most useful governance artifact in the field, and regulators and reviewers both
recognize it.

Your datasheet for TinyStories is in `datasheet.md` in this folder. It follows the
standard sections: motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, uses,
distribution, maintenance. You will fill in the measured parts (sizes, counts, PII
findings) from the audit script in Exercise A. A datasheet with numbers you measured is
evidence. A datasheet copied from a web page is a summary.

## 4. The regulation hook: EU AI Act Article 10 and GDPR purpose limitation

### EU AI Act, Article 10: data and data governance

Article 10 applies to providers of **high-risk AI systems**. It says training, validation,
and testing datasets must be governed by data management practices appropriate to the
intended purpose, and must meet quality criteria. The article names eight practices in
Article 10(2):

1. (a) the design choices behind the system,
2. (b) data collection processes and the origin of the data, and for personal data the
   original purpose of collection,
3. (c) data preparation operations such as annotation, labelling, cleaning, updating,
   enrichment, and aggregation,
4. (d) the assumptions made, in particular about what the data is meant to measure or
   represent,
5. (e) an assessment of the availability, quantity, and suitability of the data,
6. (f) examination for possible biases that could affect health and safety, harm
   fundamental rights, or lead to discrimination prohibited under EU law, especially
   where outputs influence future inputs,
7. (g) measures to detect, prevent, and mitigate the biases found in (f),
8. (h) identification of data gaps and shortcomings, and how they are addressed.

Article 10(3) then sets the quality bar: datasets must be **relevant, sufficiently
representative, and to the best extent possible free of errors and complete**, with
appropriate statistical properties for the intended purpose.

Article 10(5) is the exception you met in Chapter 1: providers may process special-category
data (GDPR Article 9 data such as race or health) **only** to the extent strictly necessary
for bias detection and correction, only if that cannot be done with anonymized or synthetic
data, and only with safeguards. It is a narrow door, not a general permission. Chapter 12
works through it in full.

Timing note: the high-risk duties in Chapter III of the Act were originally set to apply
from August 2, 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI moved the application date for standalone
high-risk systems to December 2, 2027, with systems embedded in regulated products
following later. The requirements themselves did not change, only the date. Check the
current date when you rely on this.

### GDPR, Article 5(1)(b): purpose limitation

GDPR Article 5(1)(b) says personal data must be collected for **specified, explicit, and
legitimate purposes** and not further processed in a way that is incompatible with those
purposes. That is purpose limitation, and it bites training data in two places:

- **Collection purpose vs training purpose.** Data collected to run a service (say, support
  chat logs) was not collected to train a model. Using it for training is a new purpose,
  and you need a lawful basis for it under Article 6, plus a compatibility check under
  Article 6(4). The factors in Article 6(4) are the link between the purposes, the context
  of collection, the nature of the data, the consequences for the person, and the safeguards
  you apply.
- **Research is not a free pass.** Article 5(1)(b) says further processing for scientific
  research is not automatically incompatible, but only with the safeguards in Article 89(1):
  technical and organizational measures such as pseudonymization, data minimization, and
  limits on identification. "It is research" does not excuse skipping the safeguards.

The practical test, which you will run in Exercise B: write down the original purpose in
one sentence, write down your purpose in one sentence, and ask whether a reasonable person
whose data this is would expect the second from the first. If the answer is no, you need a
new lawful basis or you do not use the data.

### How the two laws fit together

Article 10 tells you what good data governance looks like. GDPR tells you whether you may
process the personal data at all. Article 10(2)(b) even points back to GDPR: for personal
data, you must record the original purpose of collection. A dataset that passes Article 10
but fails GDPR purpose limitation is still unusable. Your audit checks both.

## Key terms (first-use glossary)

- **Datasheet for datasets:** a standard document describing a dataset's motivation,
  composition, collection, and recommended uses (Gebru et al., 2021).
- **License:** the permission that states who may use data, for what, and under what
  conditions. No license means no permission granted.
- **Share-alike:** a license condition (as in CC BY-SA) requiring derivatives to be shared
  under the same license.
- **Purpose limitation:** GDPR Article 5(1)(b): data collected for one purpose must not be
  further processed incompatibly with it.
- **Lawful basis:** GDPR Article 6: the legal ground (consent, contract, legitimate
  interests, and others) that makes processing personal data lawful.
- **Article 10(2):** the eight data governance practices the EU AI Act requires for
  high-risk AI training, validation, and testing datasets.
- **Provenance:** the recorded chain of where data came from and what happened to it.
